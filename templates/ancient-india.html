🪷 Ancient Indian Philosophy

A tradition that moved from ritual hymns to introspective metaphysics, and eventually gave rise to two movements — Buddhism and Jainism — that would go on to shape belief across all of Asia.

Introduction

Indian philosophy developed along a path distinct from — though in places strikingly similar to — the Greek tradition. Where Greek thought largely began by asking what the physical world is made of, Indian philosophy from its earliest period was already entangled with questions of ritual, cosmic order, and the self's place within a much larger, cyclical universe.

This page traces that development across three broad chapters: the ritual world of the Vedas, the introspective turn of the Upanishads toward the nature of the self, and the emergence of Buddhism and Jainism as heterodox movements that broke from Vedic orthodoxy entirely. Unlike Greek philosophy, much of this material is anonymous and communal, transmitted orally for centuries before being written down — so the dates below reflect general scholarly ranges rather than precise, agreed-upon years.

🏺 India Before Philosophy

Long before the Vedas were composed, the Indian subcontinent was home to the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 3300–1300 BCE), one of the world's earliest urban societies, with sophisticated cities, trade networks, and a still-undeciphered script. Its religious and philosophical life remains largely a mystery to modern scholars, and it is not directly continuous with the Vedic tradition that follows it and that ancient Indian philosophy is usually traced back to.

The Vedic period that followed was organized around clans of pastoralists and agriculturalists who spoke an early form of Sanskrit, and whose religious life centered on fire sacrifice (yajna), performed by a specialized priestly class to honor a pantheon of deities associated with natural forces — Agni (fire), Indra (storms and war), Varuna (cosmic law). Ritual, in this world, was not incidental to religious life; it was believed to actively sustain ṛta, the cosmic order itself, making the priest's correct performance of sacrifice a matter of universal, not merely local, consequence.

It was within this ritual-centered world — and eventually in reaction against parts of it — that the philosophical literature explored below took shape, beginning with the hymns of the Vedas themselves.

1️⃣ The Vedic Period (c. 1500–800 BCE)

The Foundational Scriptures

The Vedas are the oldest layer of Indian religious and philosophical literature, composed over several centuries and traditionally divided into four collections — the Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda, and Atharvaveda — with the Rigveda, a collection of hymns to various deities, generally considered the oldest. At their core, the Vedas are liturgical texts: hymns, chants, and ritual instructions used by priests to conduct sacrifices intended to maintain cosmic and social order.

Philosophically, the Vedas matter less for explicit argument than for the questions they first raise about the origin of the universe and humanity's relationship to a cosmic order beyond human control. The Nasadiya Sukta, or "Hymn of Creation," found in the Rigveda, is often cited as one of the earliest expressions of philosophical skepticism, ending with the striking admission that perhaps not even the gods know how the universe truly began — a note of genuine uncertainty embedded within an otherwise confident ritual tradition.

2️⃣ The Upanishadic Turn (c. 800–200 BCE)

From Ritual to Inquiry

The Upanishads mark a decisive shift in Indian thought — away from the ritual focus of the earlier Vedic hymns and toward direct philosophical inquiry into the nature of reality, the self, and liberation. The texts are structured largely as dialogues between sages and students, or between a teacher and a questioning king, the term Upanishad itself often translated as "sitting near" — a student sitting close to a teacher to receive advanced or secret knowledge.

The central philosophical achievement of the Upanishads is the identification of Atman, the innermost self of an individual, with Brahman, the single, unchanging ultimate reality underlying the entire universe — most famously expressed in the phrase "Tat tvam asi" ("That thou art") from the Chandogya Upanishad. This claim would become the philosophical foundation of the later school of Advaita (non-dual) Vedanta.

The Upanishads also give their clearest early articulation to concepts that would define nearly all later Indian philosophy: karma (actions shaping future existence), samsara (the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth), and moksha (liberation from that cycle through realizing the self's true nature). Where the Vedas asked how to maintain cosmic order through ritual, the Upanishads ask a more introspective question: what is the self performing the ritual, and can it be freed from rebirth altogether?

3️⃣ Buddhism & Jainism (c. 6th–5th Century BCE)

The Heterodox Movements

Around the 6th and 5th centuries BCE, two movements emerged that broke directly with Vedic orthodoxy rather than reinterpreting it from within, as the Upanishads largely had. Siddhartha Gautama, later called the Buddha ("the awakened one"), taught the Four Noble Truths — that life inherently involves suffering; that suffering arises from craving; that suffering can cease; and that the way to end it is the Noble Eightfold Path. Crucially, Buddhism denies the existence of any permanent self at all, a doctrine known as anatta, standing in direct contrast to the Upanishadic teaching of an eternal Atman identical with Brahman.

Around the same period, Mahavira, traditionally regarded as the 24th and final Tirthankara of the current cosmic cycle, revived and reformed the older tradition of Jainism. Its central commitment is ahimsa — non-violence extended to all living creatures — paired with the epistemological doctrine of anekantavada ("many-sidedness"), the view that reality is genuinely complex and that any single perspective can capture only part of the truth.

Both movements rejected the authority of the Vedas and the priestly caste that performed their rituals, offering instead paths to liberation open to anyone willing to follow their ethical and meditative disciplines — a genuinely radical proposition in a world still organized around inherited ritual privilege.

👤 Major Thinkers

The key figures behind each chapter of this era, in brief.

Gautama Buddha

c. 563–483 BCE

Taught the Four Noble Truths and the doctrine of anatta (non-self), founding Buddhism.

Mahavira

c. 599–527 BCE

Reviver of Jainism; taught absolute non-violence (ahimsa) and anekantavada.

Yajnavalkya

c. 8th century BCE

Sage central to the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad's teachings on the self and liberation.

Adi Shankaracharya

c. 8th century CE

Developed Advaita Vedanta, teaching the non-dual nature of Brahman and the ultimate unity of Atman and Brahman.

Ramanuja

c. 1017–1137 CE

Developed Vishishtadvaita Vedanta, emphasizing the relationship between Brahman, individual souls, and the material world.

Nagarjuna

c. 150–250 CE

Developed Madhyamaka philosophy, emphasizing the concept of emptiness (shunyata) and the dependent nature of reality.

💡 Major Ideas & Discoveries

  • 🕉 Atman–Brahman — the identity of the individual self with ultimate reality
  • 🔄 Karma & Samsara — action shaping the cycle of rebirth
  • 🕊 Moksha — liberation from the cycle of rebirth
  • 🚫 Anatta — the Buddhist denial of a permanent self
  • 🙏 Ahimsa — non-violence toward all living beings
  • 👁 Anekantavada — the many-sidedness of truth

🌍 Influence on Later Philosophy

The Upanishadic vision of Atman and Brahman went on to become the philosophical foundation of the later classical school of Advaita Vedanta, systematized centuries afterward by Adi Shankaracharya, and remains a living tradition in Hindu philosophy and practice today. Buddhism, meanwhile, spread far beyond its place of origin — across the Indian subcontinent, into Sri Lanka, Central Asia, and eventually China, Korea, and Japan — splitting along the way into major branches including Theravada and Mahayana, and profoundly shaping the philosophy, art, and culture of nearly every society it reached.

Jain philosophy's uncompromising commitment to non-violence would resurface many centuries later as a direct influence on Mahatma Gandhi's philosophy of nonviolent resistance, itself a major influence on 20th-century civil rights and independence movements well beyond India. In this sense, questions first raised in Vedic fire rituals and Upanishadic dialogues traveled forward not only through religious and philosophical tradition, but into modern political thought as well.
