Metaphysics

The branch of philosophy that asks what reality truly is — what exists, what it means to be, and how existence, time, space, causation, and identity shape the world we experience.

What is Metaphysics

Metaphysics is the branch of philosophy that investigates the fundamental nature of reality — what exists, what it means for something to exist, and how the things that exist relate to one another. Where physics asks how the universe behaves, metaphysics asks a more basic question underneath it: what kind of thing is the universe, and what is really real?

The name itself has a slightly accidental history — it comes from the Greek ta meta ta physika, "the things after the physics," referring simply to where Aristotle's writings on first philosophy were shelved after his works on nature. But the name stuck, and came to describe exactly the kind of inquiry Aristotle was engaged in: questions that lie beneath or beyond what any physical science can settle — the nature of substance, causation, identity, time, and possibility itself.

Because metaphysics deals with questions that cannot be settled by observation or experiment alone, it proceeds instead through careful argument, thought experiment, and conceptual analysis. It is often considered the most abstract branch of philosophy — and also, for many philosophers, the most foundational, since almost every other branch of philosophy quietly assumes some metaphysical position before it can even get started.

Core Questions Metaphysics Asks

Metaphysics organizes itself around a handful of deep, interlocking questions:

  • What does it mean for something to exist? Do abstract things like numbers or justice exist in the same way physical objects do?
  • What are things ultimately made of? Is reality fundamentally physical, mental, or some combination of both?
  • What makes something the same thing over time? If every part of an object is replaced, is it still the same object?
  • Is the future open or fixed? Do human beings have genuine free will, or is every event, including our choices, determined by prior causes?
  • What is the nature of time and space? Are they real features of the universe, or frameworks the mind imposes to make sense of experience?

These questions rarely admit of experimental proof, which is part of what makes metaphysics both frustrating and endlessly productive as a field of inquiry.

History of Metaphysics

Metaphysical inquiry begins in earnest with the Pre-Socratic philosophers, who asked what single underlying substance the world was made of — water, according to Thales; fire, according to Heraclitus, who also argued that all things are in constant flux. Parmenides took the opposite view entirely, arguing that change is an illusion and that true reality is a single, unchanging whole. Plato resolved this tension by proposing two levels of reality: the imperfect physical world we perceive, and a higher realm of perfect, unchanging Forms that physical things merely imitate.

Aristotle rejected Plato's separate realm of Forms, arguing instead that form and matter are inseparably united in the particular things we encounter in the world. Centuries later, Descartes reopened the question of what is fundamentally real by proposing a strict division between mind and matter — a position known as dualism, that reshaped metaphysics for the next several hundred years. Kant then argued that human beings can never know reality as it truly is in itself, only as it appears to us through the structures of our own minds — a claim that continues to divide metaphysicians into realist and anti-realist camps to this day.

Major Metaphysical Positions

Materialism (also called physicalism) holds that everything that exists is physical — matter, energy, and their interactions. On this view, mental states like thoughts and feelings are ultimately nothing more than processes occurring in the physical brain.

Idealism takes the opposite position, holding that reality is fundamentally mental — that the physical world, or our knowledge of it, ultimately depends on mind rather than existing independently of it. George Berkeley famously argued that objects only exist insofar as they are perceived.

Dualism, most closely associated with Descartes, holds that mind and matter are two distinct and irreducible kinds of substance — the physical body operating according to physical law, and the mind or soul operating according to entirely different principles, with the two somehow interacting.

Realism about universals holds that general properties — redness, roundness, justice — genuinely exist, independent of the particular objects that instantiate them. Nominalism denies this, arguing that only particular, individual things exist, and that general categories are simply useful labels the mind applies to them.

Determinism holds that every event, including every human decision, is the necessary result of prior causes, leaving no room for genuine free will. Libertarian free will (a metaphysical position, distinct from the political philosophy of the same name) denies this, holding that human beings possess a genuine capacity to choose otherwise than they do.

Metaphysics and Science

Metaphysics and physical science are often treated as rivals — one speculative, the other empirical — but the relationship is far more entangled than that division suggests. Every scientific theory rests on metaphysical assumptions it cannot itself prove: that the universe is orderly, that causes reliably produce effects, that the future will resemble the past in relevant ways. Science can test hypotheses within those assumptions; it cannot use experiment to justify the assumptions themselves.

Modern physics has, if anything, made metaphysics more urgent rather than less. Quantum mechanics raises genuine metaphysical puzzles about whether reality has a definite state before it is observed, and whether causation at the smallest scales still behaves the way classical determinism assumed. Debates over the nature of time, the reality of possible worlds, and whether mathematical objects exist independently of physical reality remain squarely metaphysical questions that no experiment alone can settle — physics tells us how the universe behaves; it takes metaphysics to ask what that behavior tells us about what is ultimately real.

Metaphysics in Everyday Life

Metaphysical assumptions rarely announce themselves, yet they quietly shape ordinary thought and language. Asking whether a renovated childhood home is still "the same house," debating whether a person who has fundamentally changed their values and memories is "still the same person," or wondering whether a difficult choice was ever really avoidable — all of these are everyday brushes with genuinely metaphysical questions about identity, change, and freedom.

Grief, too, often carries an implicit metaphysics — questions about whether identity, memory, or consciousness persist beyond physical death sit at the heart of how different cultures and individuals make sense of loss. Even casual conversation about fate, coincidence, or whether "things happen for a reason" invokes long-standing metaphysical debates about determinism and causation. Studying metaphysics does not settle these questions definitively, but it offers sharper tools for noticing when they are being asked at all.

Famous Thought Experiments

Because metaphysical questions resist direct empirical testing, philosophers have long relied on carefully constructed thought experiments to sharpen intuitions and expose hidden assumptions.

The Ship of Theseus, dating back to Plutarch, imagines a ship whose planks are replaced one by one over time until none of the original material remains. Is it still the same ship? The puzzle becomes even sharper if the discarded original planks are collected and reassembled elsewhere — which of the two resulting ships, if either, is the "real" one? The problem forces a choice about what identity over time actually consists in: material continuity, structural form, or something else entirely.

The Brain in a Vat thought experiment, a modern descendant of Descartes' evil demon argument, imagines a brain kept alive in a vat of nutrients, with electrical signals fed into it that perfectly simulate ordinary experience. The brain would have no way of distinguishing this simulated existence from ordinary reality. The scenario raises an unsettling question that later inspired films like The Matrix: how can any of us be certain that our own experience of the world is not similarly manufactured?

METAPHYSICIANS

Key Thinkers

The figures whose ideas shaped how we still think about reality, existence, and identity.

Parmenides

Argued that true reality is a single, unchanging, indivisible whole, and that the change we perceive through the senses is ultimately an illusion — a claim that set the terms for metaphysical debate for centuries.

"What is, is; and what is not, cannot be."

René Descartes

Established modern mind-body dualism, arguing that thinking substance and extended, physical substance are fundamentally distinct kinds of being, connected but never reducible to one another.

"I think, therefore I am."

Immanuel Kant

Argued that the human mind actively structures experience through categories like space, time, and causation — meaning reality as it appears to us can never be identical to reality as it exists "in itself."

"We can know a priori of things only what we ourselves put into them."
